Understanding AI's Role

Artificial Intelligence (AI) has rapidly become an essential part of modern life, powering everything from virtual assistants and recommendation systems to medical diagnostics and self-driving cars. By automating tasks, analyzing vast amounts of data, and improving efficiency, AI offers numerous benefits. However, as AI continues to evolve, it's important to examine its environmental impact, particularly its energy consumption and resource demands.

High Energy Consumption

Data centers supporting AI systems consume massive amounts of electricity. How it works is that when a user asks an AI chatbot a query, that information gets routed to a data center, which then uses an AI system there to come up with solutions. This takes a lot of energy. A data center is a physical location where the physical hardware and technology of a company resides. Moreover, it receives and sends our data, in between those steps, putting their technology to use on the data. Why does AI take so much computing power? Key 3 factors that cause AI to take so much computing power: amount of data that the model is trained on, the complexity of the model, and the amount of requests from users. When a model is prompted a question, it must first interpret the user’s input, then also create a valid output. Also, GPUs are running 24 hours a day while the model is being trained on its data.

Carbon Footprint

Training large AI models emits significant amounts of CO₂—comparable to multiple gasoline cars over their lifetime. Every time an AI system is trained or used at scale, it consumes electricity, often sourced from non-renewable energy. For example, training a single large AI model can emit over 280,000 kg of CO₂, which is equivalent to the lifetime emissions of five cars.

The carbon footprint of AI depends on:

  • The amount of data the model is trained on
  • The complexity of the model architecture
  • The frequency and volume of user requests

These emissions are driven by the computational power required by GPUs and TPUs running 24/7 in massive cloud data centers. Unless powered by renewable energy, these data centers rely on fossil fuels and contribute heavily to global emissions.

Tools like Input–Output Analysis (IOA) are helping scientists trace carbon emissions across industries. With more transparency from tech companies and investment in green infrastructure, the carbon footprint of AI can be significantly reduced.

Water Usage for Cooling

With the growth of generative AI, companies have raised their water usage due to the demand for cooling data centers. With the use of cooling towers and air mechanisms to dissipate heat, these companies can use up to 9 liters of water to evaporate per kWh of energy used. AI models, especially large ones like GPT-3 and GPT-4, require significant computational power, which in turn generates a lot of heat. To prevent overheating, data centers use cooling systems that consume large amounts of water. Cooling towers, for instance, rely on water evaporation to produce cold water, which is essential for maintaining optimal temperatures.

Electronic Waste (E-Waste) & Resource Extraction

AI-driven demand for advanced chips and hardware leads to frequent upgrades and electronic waste. Mining for rare earth metals (e.g., lithium, cobalt) required for AI hardware causes deforestation, pollution, and habitat destruction. The improper disposal and recycling of e-waste can release harmful/toxic substances like lead and mercury into the environment, posing significant health risks, especially to vulnerable populations such as children and pregnant women. Implementing sustainable practices in both e-waste management and resource extraction can reduce environmental impact. This includes proper recycling methods and the use of eco-friendly technologies.

Many unsound practices have been observed at e-waste sites including:

  • scavenging
  • dumping on land or in water bodies
  • landfilling along with regular waste
  • opening burning or heating
  • acid baths or acid leaching
  • stripping and shredding plastic coatings
